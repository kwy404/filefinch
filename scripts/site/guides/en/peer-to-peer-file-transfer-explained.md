Peer to peer file transfer means two devices send a file straight to each other, instead of one uploading it to a server and the other downloading it later. The idea is simple. Making it work across home routers, mobile networks and office firewalls is where the interesting parts are.

This guide explains those parts in plain words: what NAT is and why it gets in the way, what STUN, TURN and ICE do, what signaling means, and how a WebRTC data channel carries your file. It uses filefinch, a free open source browser app, as the running example, but the ideas apply to any tool built on WebRTC.

## Client server versus peer to peer

Most file sharing is client server. Your device is the client, a company's computer is the server, and every file passes through that server. It is like sending a parcel through a post office that keeps it at the counter until the recipient comes to collect it.

Peer to peer is more like handing the parcel over in person. Both devices are equals, or peers, and the data goes from one to the other. That brings some clear benefits:

- No stored copy on a third party machine
- No upload that has to finish before the download can start
- On the same local network, the data may never leave the building

It also brings one clear requirement: both devices have to be online at the same time, because nobody in the middle is holding the parcel.

## The obstacle: NAT and firewalls

Your phone or laptop usually does not have its own public internet address. Your router has one public address and shares it among all the devices in your home, giving each one a private address such as 192.168.1.23. This is called Network Address Translation, or NAT.

NAT works well for normal browsing, because your device starts every conversation and the router remembers where to send the replies. It is a problem for peer to peer, because now two devices, each hidden behind its own router, need to reach each other. Neither one knows the other's public address, and each router tends to drop traffic it was not expecting.

Firewalls add another layer. Home routers are usually relaxed about outgoing traffic. Corporate networks and some mobile carriers are much stricter.

## STUN: finding out how you look from outside

The first trick is simple: ask someone outside. A STUN server is a small public service that answers one question, namely which address and port a message came from. Your browser sends a tiny request, and the answer tells it how it appears on the public internet.

filefinch uses public STUN servers run by Google for this step. The STUN server sees your IP address, as any server you contact does, but it never sees your files. Its only job is to report your public address back to you.

Once each device knows its public address, the two can often open a path through both routers by sending packets toward each other at roughly the same time. This technique, usually called hole punching, works on most home networks. The STUN protocol is described in [RFC 8489](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8489).

## TURN: the relay for difficult networks

Some networks make a direct path impossible. Symmetric NAT, the carrier grade NAT used by some mobile carriers, and strict corporate firewalls can all prevent hole punching. In those cases a TURN server can help. TURN is a relay: both devices connect to it, and it forwards traffic between them.

A few important points about TURN:

- The data stays encrypted between the two browsers, so the relay forwards packets it cannot read.
- The relay can see both IP addresses and how much data flows.
- Relays cost money to run, because every byte passes through them. That is why free public TURN servers are rare. Google, for example, offers public STUN servers but no public TURN server.

filefinch detects when a direct connection fails, explains what happened in plain words, and offers a Settings option to add your own TURN servers, which are stored only on your device. TURN is described in [RFC 8656](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8656).

## ICE: trying every path and picking one that works

With several possible routes, something has to choose. ICE, short for Interactive Connectivity Establishment, is that referee. Each device collects a list of candidates, which are addresses where it might be reachable:

| Candidate type | What it is | When it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Host | The device's own local network address | Both devices on the same network |
| Server reflexive | The public address learned through STUN | Most home networks |
| Relay | An address on a TURN server | When nothing else gets through |

The two devices swap their candidate lists, test pairs of candidates against each other, and settle on a pair that works. Direct paths are preferred, and the relay is the last resort. ICE is described in [RFC 8445](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8445).

This is also why two devices on the same Wi-Fi can connect without any internet once filefinch has been loaded: the host candidates are enough. That works with the invite link mode described below, since the 6 word mode needs internet to reach its relays. The [same Wi-Fi guide](guide:transfer-files-on-the-same-wifi) covers that case.

## Signaling: the introduction

There is a chicken and egg problem in all of this. To swap candidate lists and security details, the devices need to talk. But they cannot talk until they are connected. The exchange that solves this is called signaling. In WebRTC terms, one device writes an offer (its candidates plus the details needed to secure the connection), and the other replies with an answer.

WebRTC does not dictate how signaling happens. Many apps run their own signaling server that passes these setup messages along. filefinch runs no server of its own, and it offers two ways to carry the offer and the answer instead.

### The default: 6 words and public relays

When you tap **Show pairing code**, the first device shows 6 words, with a QR code beside them that holds the same words. The words come from the English list of BIP39, a published standard with 2048 simple words. On the second device you type the words, where the first four letters of each are enough and single typos are fixed, or you scan the QR code. Both routes end in the same handshake:

1. Each device turns the 6 words into two things: a topic name, which works as a meeting point and looks random, and a secret key.
2. Both devices connect to several public MQTT brokers. MQTT is a lightweight messaging protocol, widely used by sensors and smart home gadgets, in which a broker passes messages between clients that follow the same topic. These brokers are run by third parties, not by filefinch.
3. The first device posts its offer to the topic, sealed with the key. The second device opens it and posts its answer, sealed the same way.
4. As soon as the connection opens, both devices leave the brokers. The code works once and expires after 10 minutes.
5. The device that joined shows 6 new confirmation words, and you type them on the first device. No file moves until they match.

In short, the 6-word handshake travels encrypted through public relays that cannot read it. A broker sees a random looking topic name and ciphertext, and like any server you connect to, it sees the IP address that connected. It never sees your files. Those go device to device over WebRTC, exactly as in any other peer to peer connection. Using several brokers at once helps when one of them is slow or offline. If none answers, the link mode below is the fallback.

### Zero third parties: the link carries the handshake

The other option removes the relays from the handshake entirely. You choose "Prefer zero third parties? Use a link or QR instead", and the setup details travel inside a link that you pass along yourself:

1. The first device gathers all of its candidates before doing anything else, so the complete offer is ready in one piece.
2. It trims the offer, compresses it and encodes it into a link and a QR code. This is the invite.
3. The second device opens the link or scans the QR code, and creates its answer as a short reply code, again shown as a link or QR code.
4. The reply goes back through any messenger, or by showing it to the first device's camera.

One round trip, and the two browsers can connect, with no relay carrying the handshake. Like every mode, each browser still asks a public STUN server for its own public address, and a TURN relay may be used when both networks block direct routes. This mode works fully offline on a local network once the app is loaded. If camera scanning is not available in a browser, pasting the codes by hand always works. Because the invite contains your network addresses, share it only with your own device or the person you are sending to.

## Data channels: carrying the file

Once ICE has found a path, WebRTC sets up an encrypted connection. Encryption is not optional in WebRTC: it always uses DTLS, with keys generated in the two browsers for that one connection. On top of it runs a data channel, a pipe for any kind of data. Video calls mostly use other parts of WebRTC; file transfer uses data channels.

Here is how filefinch uses a data channel to move a file:

- The sender reads the file in 64 KiB chunks, so the whole file is never loaded into memory at once.
- The chunks travel through the channel, and the receiver saves them as they arrive.
- The file is hashed with SHA-256 as it streams, and the receiver checks the hash at the end.
- If the connection drops, the transfer resumes from the last confirmed offset after you reconnect.

For extra protection, both people can type the same passphrase. Each device then derives an AES-GCM key from it, and every chunk is sealed again before it is sent. The passphrase itself never travels.

### How do you know nobody is in the middle?

In the word mode, the offer and answer cross public relays sealed with a key that only someone holding the 6 words can derive. In the link mode, the invite and the reply travel through channels you choose. In either case, someone able to intercept and replace the setup details could, in principle, put themselves in the middle. filefinch makes that visible. In the link mode, both devices show a short connection fingerprint, a 12 digit code derived from the DTLS certificates of both sides, and you compare it on both screens. In the word mode, filefinch hashes both certificate fingerprints and a session nonce with SHA-256 and turns the result into 6 confirmation words. The device that joined shows them, and you type them on the device that showed the pairing code. If the code or the words match, nobody swapped the setup details on the way. A device that joined with a code it saw over someone's shoulder has its own certificate, so it gets different words, and three wrong tries close the connection. The guide [is WebRTC file sharing safe](guide:is-webrtc-file-sharing-safe) goes deeper into this.

## When peer to peer is not the right tool

Peer to peer transfer, including filefinch, does not fit every job:

- **Both devices must be online at once.** If the receiver is offline, a cloud upload lets the file wait for them.
- **Speed follows the slower side.** Across the internet that is usually the sender's upload speed. On the same local network it is often much faster.
- **Some networks need a relay.** Without a TURN server, a few strict networks cannot connect at all.
- **The word mode needs the public relays.** Without internet, or if no relay answers, use an invite link instead.
- **Mobile browsers may pause background tabs.** Keep the screen on during long transfers.
